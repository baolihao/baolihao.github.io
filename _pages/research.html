1. Learning Interaction Laws and Collective Dynamics

Our research focuses on learning the governing mechanisms of collective behavior from data, with particular emphasis on discovering interaction kernels in agent-based dynamical systems. These systems arise in diverse applications, including swarming, flocking, synchronization, and social dynamics. Central challenges include inferring interaction laws from severely limited observations such as single steady-state snapshots, and identifying the unknown neighborhood structures through which agents interact.

1a. Learning from Equilibrium Patterns

A distinctive feature of many collective systems is that they converge to structured steady states (ring formations, crystal lattices, or flocking configurations) where time derivatives vanish. Learning from such static snapshots is fundamentally ill-conditioned: the governing equation reduces to a homogeneous system, and interaction kernels are identifiable only up to scaling. We develop a learning framework that addresses this ill-posedness through distribution-based regularization, energy-based sign determination from the gradient flow structure, and a stopping-time strategy for time-scale recovery. Our approach enables stable kernel reconstruction from single time snapshots under noisy and limited data, and extends to multi-species and second-order systems.

1b. Learning under Topological and Adaptive Neighborhoods

Classical interaction models assume all-to-all coupling, but in many real systems agents interact only with local neighbors defined by adaptive criteria such as KNN, Voronoi tessellations, or density-weighted neighborhoods. We develop a unified data-driven framework that simultaneously learns the interaction kernel and identifies the appropriate neighborhood structure, encompassing models with 1/N, 1/φ, and 1/χ normalizations. The framework handles both deterministic and stochastic dynamics, including Langevin, self-propelled particle, and Vicsek-type systems, with validation on simulated and real-world datasets.

2. Mathematical Modeling of Urban Crime Dynamics

This research investigates how spatio-temporal crime patterns emerge from individual-level interactions, with a focus on the formation, persistence, and suppression of crime hotspots. Building on nonlinear PDE models for residential burglary derived from agent-based dynamics via the mean-field limit, we study their behavior in realistic urban settings with heterogeneous parameters and complex geometries.

We developed a finite element framework enforcing natural boundary conditions with an efficient iterative decoupled scheme, validated against agent-based simulations on the city of Chicago. The framework captures key phenomena including hotspot nucleation under weak neighborhood effects, spatial heterogeneity driven by infrastructure (e.g., highways), and the quantitative dependence of hotspot size and number on model parameters. Published in Mathematical Models and Methods in Applied Sciences with open-source code.

Ongoing work extends this framework to a coupled four-variable PDE system incorporating adaptive law enforcement dynamics. In this model, police density evolves via a biased random walk toward high-crime regions, driven by a time-delayed crime signal. Linear stability analysis reveals that the coupling between crime and enforcement induces emergent oscillatory dynamics at the macroscopic level. These oscillations arise from feedback loops between attractiveness, criminal density, and delayed police response, highlighting the role of time delays in shaping complex urban patterns.

3. Physics-Informed Neural Networks

We developed data-driven solvers based on Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) that explicitly enforce physical constraints to mitigate ill-conditioning and spectral bias in stiff, time-dependent partial differential equations. The work systematically investigates hard enforcement of initial conditions and adaptive loss weighting to address the instability commonly encountered in multiscale and rapidly evolving dynamics.

We conducted a large-scale ablation study across representative stiff PDE systems arising in materials science and mathematical biology, including Allen–Cahn, Cahn–Hilliard, and Gray–Scott equations, etc. The study quantifies how precise constraint enforcement and dynamically balanced loss terms improve training robustness, accuracy, and convergence efficiency, particularly in regimes characterized by sharp gradients and disparate temporal scales. Extensive numerical benchmarks demonstrate that the proposed training strategies significantly enhance PINN performance for stiff and chaotic dynamics, offering practical guidance for reliable physics-constrained learning in challenging PDE settings. 4. Ion Transport and Poisson–Nernst–Planck Models

Another strand of my research concerns ion transport in confined systems, modeled by Poisson–Nernst–Planck equations with steric effects, cross-diffusion , and dynamic boundary conditions . These models are motivated by applications in electrochemistry and biological transport.

We focus on the mathematical analysis of these systems, including energy identities, dissipation mechanisms, and the global existence of weak solutions. By incorporating physically meaningful boundary dynamics, this work improves the fidelity of continuum models for charged species transport near interfaces.
